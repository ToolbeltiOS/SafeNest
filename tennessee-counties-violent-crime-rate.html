1 jurisdiction(s) withheld from this table. Property offences outnumber violent offences by roughly five to one across this dataset. A jurisdiction reporting fewer property than violent offences is reporting something other than a full year of both, so it is excluded rather than ranked — and named here rather than removed quietly:

  • Macon County Sheriff — reported fewer property offences than violent (64.0 vs 216.7 per 100k)

Read the scope before the numbers. Each row is a county sheriff's office, not a county. A sheriff reports crime for the area it polices — in most Tennessee counties that is primarily unincorporated land, because residents of incorporated cities are policed by their own municipal departments and counted separately. These figures therefore do not describe every resident of the named county, and a county's sheriff rate should not be compared against a city rate as though the two covered the same population.

Why the rankings look this way

Sheriff jurisdictions vary enormously in reporting population, and a rate computed over a small population moves sharply on a small number of offenses. Rows near the top and bottom of this table are the most sensitive to that effect.

A limit on what these figures can tell you

Agencies report to the FBI voluntarily, and the underlying data carries no field stating how many months of the year each agency actually reported. We can therefore detect an agency whose figures are obviously partial — the withheld rows above — but we cannot certify that every remaining row represents a full twelve months. A jurisdiction that under-reported both violent and property offences in proportion would look ordinary here. Treat these as reported totals, which is what they are, rather than as complete counts of what occurred.
